Before anything else

First, the short version

You can do this. Today.

There is nothing in these instructions that you can get so wrong that we can't fix it together. Not one thing. If you open your computer right now and get started, you are already doing it right.

So read this at whatever speed you want. Then go open your computer.

What three to four hours looks like

GOAL asks for about three to four hours of school work a day. That's the number. How you get there is up to you.

Some people work best in one long stretch. Some people need to break it up. Both are fine. Here are real schedules that work:

  • Work 8:00 to noon, straight through. OR
  • Work 30 minutes, break 15 minutes, work 30 minutes, then lunch. Repeat. OR
  • Work an hour, take an hour walk, work an hour, nap, then work two hours. OR
  • Work one hour at 8:00, one at noon, and two at 9:00 at night. OR
  • If you have a job, do two hours before a shift and two after. OR
  • Work 9:00 at night to midnight, then an hour the next morning. OR
  • Some other set up.

You know yourself better than I do. The only rule is be honest with yourself. "I work better at night" is a real thing. "I'll do it later" every single day is not the same thing, and you already know the difference.

Just put in the time and make it add up. Ever forward.

One small tip

Turning your computer off at night and back on in the morning is not the same as restarting it. Windows takes a shortcut when it shuts down. Once a week or so, click the power menu and pick Restart instead of Shut down. It clears things out and fixes a surprising number of weird problems.

The strip along the bottom of your screen is called the taskbar. The little pictures on it are called icons.

Icons don't always have names under them. If you're not sure what one is, hover your mouse over it and wait a second. A little label will pop up and tell you.

Teams is the two purple people.

One small thing about Teams

Sometimes when you open Teams on your computer, it might say it's downloading and then… just sit there. It does nothing. It's stuck.

Don't worry about it

This happens sometimes. We'll always be able to get to Teams through your browser (Edge) instead, and that's the easiest way to get there anyway. So if the Teams app gets stuck, just ignore it and open Edge. We'll show you how in a few cards.

A quick word about browsers

A browser is the program you use to get on the internet. Edge and Chrome are both browsers.

Here's my honest advice: use Edge for school, and Chrome for everything else.

Your Gmail, YouTube, Netflix, whatever you do for fun. Chrome is great for all of that. But school stuff works more smoothly in Edge. It signs you in better, it syncs better, and your school bookmarks are already there.

You can do school in Chrome. You'll just run into more problems, and I'd rather you spend your time on your classes than on troubleshooting.

Open your first class

You'll see all your courses in Schoology.

Start with the first class in the upper left. Or start with whichever one looks most interesting. That's allowed. Skip Homeroom for now; there usually isn't much in it.

Here's what the inside of a virtual classroom looks like before there is anything in the class. When you open yours, the classroom will look the same, but you will have material in it: lessons, videos, assignments, and so on.

Yours may look almost exactly like this, but if there are small differences, that's fine.

Look at these two things

Materials and Updates. Ninety percent of the time it's Materials.

This is also where your teacher introduces themselves and posts their syllabus.

What's a syllabus?

A syllabus is the teacher's plan for the class. It tells you what you'll learn, what programs you'll use, how grades work, and how to contact them. Read it early — it answers most of the questions you'd otherwise have to ask.

Be careful with the Grades tab

The Grades tab isn't bad. It's genuinely useful for seeing what you're missing.

But here's the trap: Grades can link you straight to an assignment and skip right past the lesson that teaches it. Students land on a quiz, think "I have no idea what any of this is, I must be terrible at this class," and get discouraged when really it's just that they never got shown the lesson.

That's not you being bad at school. That's a link taking a shortcut.

So: use Grades to find out what's missing. Then go back to Materials and get to it from there, in order.

Go in order

Top to bottom. Don't jump ahead.

There's usually a rhythm to it: lesson → assignment → quiz or test (although sometimes the lessons are inside the assignments, and you'll get used to that).

The lessons teach you the thing. The assignments and quizzes check that you got it. So if you skip the first part, the second part feels impossible for no good reason.

You can move faster than the schedule if the class lets you, and most of them do. And, crucially, if you fall behind, you can catch up. "Late" work usually has little or no penalty at GOAL, so don't hop around trying to skip stuff because you want to catch up or because you believe you already know the material.

This doesn't mean you should let yourself fall behind. But it does mean that if you are behind, there is no reason to avoid pushing forward.

8:20 AM

Your first day job: just look

On day one, you don't have to finish anything.

Open each class and read the introductory stuff. Ask yourself:

  • What's my teacher's name?
  • What programs does this class use besides Schoology?
  • Are there any introductory videos?
  • How do I contact this teacher?
  • Is there a syllabus quiz?
  • Is there anything here I don't understand?

If the answer to that last one is yes then please message me. I know I sound like a broken record, but that's what I'm here for, y'all.

The one thing I'd underline twice

Some of your classes use programs other than Schoology. Edmentum is the big one.

Your teacher will tell you if you need one, usually right at the start, and they'll tell you how to get in. If you follow their directions and still can't get in, message them — or message me and we'll do it together.

Here's why this one is crucial:

I can see your Schoology work. I can't see into Edmentum.

In Schoology I can watch your progress lesson by lesson and tell when you're moving. In Edmentum and the others, I'm blind. I can't tell if you've logged in once or never.

So a student can be working hard in Edmentum and, from where I'm sitting, look like they've vanished. Or worse, a student might not have been able to get into Edmentum at all, and I just can't tell from where I'm sitting.

That's the only reason this is a big deal. It's not because it's hard, but because I can't help you if I can't see you.

Get logged into those programs early. That's it.

And if Edmentum has a schedule built in, follow it in order, same as Schoology.

Grade reports

Every Tuesday, I send a grade report.

  • Emailed to the email addresses on file
  • Texted to the phone numbers on file

Teachers usually update grades on Friday, but I wait until Tuesday to send them out. That gives every teacher a chance to get caught up, so what you're looking at is as accurate as possible.

A quick caveat about those grades

Caveat?

A caveat is like a warning. It's like saying "This, yes, AND you should know this other thing too."

At any rate, a quick caveat about those grades:

The grades shown are a good baseline, but they aren't always 100% accurate. Some assignments might not have been entered yet, and some platforms like Edmentum and ALEKS don't always show up in the grades right away.

If the grades look roughly where you think they should be? Fantastic.

If some grades look wildly off, however, feel free to reach out to the teacher to verify everything you've turned in is there.

How to ask a teacher about a grade

Please don't open with "why is my grade so low" or anything that sounds like "hurry up and grade my paper." Instead:

First, go back into what you know you've submitted and check whether there's a record of it in your grades or a confirmation in Schoology.

Then reach out to the teacher and ask about a specific assignment.

Last thing

I've used the word crucial a few times here. I want to be clear about what I mean by it.

Crucial means this is the part that matters most. It does not mean panic. It does not mean you'd better not mess this up.

Nothing here is a test of whether you're good enough to be here. You're already here. These are just the few things that, if you do them, make everything else easier.

And, crucially, if you don't do them, I can't reach you to help. And that's all I want to do. That's what I'm here for: I'm here to help and I'm here to drink coffee.

But mostly help.

Because here's the thing: you are going to get confused about something. You're going to click the wrong link, miss an assignment, or open a quiz you weren't ready for. All of that is normal and all of it is fixable.

There is no version of this where you do something so wrong that the right answer is to give up.

So: open the computer. Check Teams. Open Schoology. Open one class.

That's the whole first day.
